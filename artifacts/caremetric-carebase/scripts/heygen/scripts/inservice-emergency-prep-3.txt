I want to close with four things that run through every emergency, no matter which one you get.

One. An accurate count of residents is the single most valuable piece of information anybody can hand a responder. It tells them whether somebody is still inside, and that decides everything they do next. Know where the current roster lives. Know it changes with admissions, discharges, hospital transfers, and residents out with family. Count against it, and report the result to whoever is coordinating, including the resident who's at an appointment, because an unexplained absence gets treated as a person missing in the building.

Two. Communication goes up the chain, fast, and it goes up as facts rather than conclusions. Tell your supervisor what you observed, not what you think it means. And do not call families yourself, and do not post anything about it. I know that sounds cold when a daughter is on the phone asking about her mother, and I've had that call. But residents' privacy doesn't pause during an emergency, and family notification comes from administration so that families get accurate information instead of six partial versions from six people.

Three. Documentation. What you observed, what you did, when, and who you notified. Facilities have reporting obligations to the Department of Human Services for certain events, and those reports get built out of what staff wrote down. If it isn't written, it effectively didn't happen.

Four. Drills are the training. Take them seriously, including the ones that land at the worst possible moment, and move at the pace you'd actually move. The entire point of a drill is to find out what doesn't work while it's still safe to find out. So when the stairwell door sticks, when a flashlight is dead, when the roster is out of date, when nobody knew who was supposed to meet the ambulance, say so in the debrief. That is not complaining. That is the exercise working.

Your facility's written plan controls over anything I've said here, and it should, because I don't know your building and it does.

What I want you to leave with is simpler than the plan. Know your building. Know which of your residents depend on something to stay well. Know your assignment. Because in every one of these events, resident safety depends far less on the plan sitting in a binder than on what the person standing in the hallway already knows.

Tonight, that person is you.
