I'm Kevin. Twenty years in senior care, five running a nursing home, seventeen in hospice. This course is about the hardest moment of your week: a resident is angry, frightened, or refusing care, and you have about three seconds to decide what to do.

Let me take something off your shoulders before we start.

When a resident yells at you, pushes your hand away, or accuses you of stealing something you never touched, that is almost never about you. Behavior is communication. For a resident with dementia, it may be the only communication they have left. What looks like aggression is usually a person telling you that they're in pain, that they need the bathroom, that they're frightened by a stranger touching them, that they don't understand what's about to happen to them, or that they've lost control of their entire day and this is the last piece of it they have.

If you can hear the message, you can usually solve the problem. If you only hear the volume, you end up in a power struggle you cannot win, and neither can they.

So where does the skill actually live? Mostly before anything happens.

It lives in knowing your residents. That this gentleman gets agitated in the late afternoon, every day, and it isn't personal. That this woman doesn't like being approached from her left side because she can't see you coming. That this one has arthritis, so being taken by the arm genuinely hurts. That knowledge sits in support plans, in shift report, and in your coworkers' heads, and every minute you spend collecting it prevents an incident later.

Then it lives in removing the triggers you can actually remove. Pain is the biggest one and the most missed, especially in residents who can't tell you they hurt. Arthritis, a pressure sore, constipation, a full bladder, a bad tooth. Any of those can show up as resistance to care or as striking out during personal care, and nobody in the room realizes it's pain.

After pain: needing the toilet. Hunger. Being too hot or cold. Noise and crowding. Being rushed. Being touched without warning. Being given three instructions at once. A caregiver they don't recognize this morning. Change any one of those and the behavior very often changes with it.

I want to be honest that none of this makes the moment easy. It makes it understandable, which is different. But understandable is what lets you stay calm, and staying calm is most of what works.
