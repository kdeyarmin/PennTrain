I want to close on the part of this topic where you contribute the most, and then on the edges where good intentions do the most damage.

What you contribute is observation. You see these residents every day. The prescriber sees them occasionally, if at all. So you're the one who notices that somebody is suddenly sleepier, more confused, unsteady on their feet, nauseated, itching, breaking out in a rash, or bruising more than they used to.

New medication plus new symptom is one of the most important patterns in senior care, and the person who catches it is almost never the prescriber. It's the person who was in the room. Report changes within days of a medication being started, stopped, or changed, and report them as observations rather than conclusions.

Some things are urgent rather than routine. Difficulty breathing. Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue. Chest pain. A sudden change in how alert somebody is. Any sign of a severe allergic reaction. Those go through your emergency process immediately, not through routine reporting.

And report errors right away, even when the resident looks completely fine. A missed dose. A doubled dose. The wrong medication. A resident who tells you they already took something you have no record of. Report it immediately instead of waiting to see whether anything happens, because the entire point is that a nurse gets to decide whether something needs to happen. And never adjust a later dose to make the math come out even. That's a medication decision, and it isn't yours.

Two other things worth naming. A resident who hoards medication, cheeks it, or shares it with a neighbor is a real danger to themselves and to the person next door, and that gets reported rather than handled quietly. And medication that appears to be missing, especially a controlled substance, gets reported immediately, because diversion is a form of resident exploitation.

Now the edges. Over-the-counter products are medications. Pain relievers, antacids, laxatives, cough and cold preparations, sleep aids, vitamins, herbal supplements, medicated creams. They carry real interactions in older adults, and several common sleep aids raise the risk of confusion and falls in exactly this population. So when a resident's daughter arrives with a bottle and asks you to leave it on the nightstand, you don't. It goes through your facility's process so it can be checked against what she's already taking. Say that kindly, because she's trying to help, and the check protects her mother.

I'll be blunt about why this course repeats every year, even for experienced staff. The mistakes in this topic almost never come from carelessness. They come from kindness. From somebody trying to help a resident who's struggling, in a hurry, with no nurse nearby. The way you protect residents, and your own job, is to know exactly where your role ends and to ask the moment you reach that edge.
