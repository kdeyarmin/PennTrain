Everything I've said so far is technique. This part is a boundary, and it does not flex for staffing, workload, or provocation. I'm going to be blunt about it.

You never use a physical restraint, and you never improvise one. Not a lap tray to keep somebody seated. Not a sheet tucked in tight. Not a chair pushed against a wall. Not holding a resident down so care can be finished. Pennsylvania regulates this narrowly, and your facility's policy will be narrower still, but forget the regulation for a second: restraints injure people, and they kill people, and they escalate the exact distress they're meant to control. If your facility has trained and authorized you in a specific approved intervention, you use it only as that training defines, only when somebody is in immediate danger, and only as long as that danger lasts.

Chemical restraint is the same answer. Medication is never used to manage a resident for staff convenience, and that includes suggesting somebody be medicated because they're difficult on your shift.

And then there's coercion, which is the one crossed most casually, usually by tired people who don't think of it as abuse. You do not threaten, shame, mock, or bargain with something a resident is entitled to. Their food. Their belongings. Their visitors. Their call bell. Their bathroom. Those are rights, not leverage. Telling a resident they won't get help until they cooperate is not a management technique. It's abuse, it's reportable, and it carries the same obligation as physical harm.

Same for ignoring a resident, talking about them like they aren't in the room, baby talk, rough handling, and retaliating against someone who complained.

When there's immediate danger, protect people first. Move other residents away. Keep your distance. Call for help the way your facility trains you to. You are allowed to leave a situation to get help. Standing there alone trying to manage it isn't bravery, and nobody is asking you for it.

Afterward, document what you observed, factually. Not that the resident was combative, which tells the next reader nothing. What was happening before it started, what they said and did, what you tried, how they responded, how it ended.

And tell somebody, because new agitation in a resident who was calm last week is one of the most common signs of a urinary tract infection, pain, or a medication problem. Behavior treated as a personality trait instead of a symptom is how a real medical problem goes undiagnosed for weeks.

Then check on yourself. Getting hit or screamed at is hard, even when you completely understand why it happened. Tell your supervisor. The people who last in this field aren't the ones who feel nothing. They're the ones who don't carry it home alone.
