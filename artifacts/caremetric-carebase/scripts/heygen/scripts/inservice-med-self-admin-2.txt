Let me give you the ones I've actually seen go wrong, because none of them involved anybody being careless.

A resident's hands shake and she keeps dropping pills. A well-meaning aide starts placing them in her mouth. That's administration.

A resident says her stomach hurts and she'll take the morning pills at lunch instead. The aide agrees and moves them. That's a timing decision, and it isn't yours to make. Report it.

A resident refuses a medication, so the aide hides it in applesauce to help. That's covert administration. It's a rights violation, and it's dangerous with medications that can't be crushed. A refusal is a resident's right. Your job is to report it, not to solve it. And it's worth asking why, because refusals often mean nausea, difficulty swallowing, a side effect they've connected to that pill, or simply not understanding what it's for.

Two residents' medications end up on the same counter. Never. One resident, one container, one task, start to finish. Never carry medications for more than one resident at a time, and never leave them where somebody else can reach them.

A pill turns up on the hallway floor. It doesn't go back in a container and it doesn't quietly go in the trash. It gets reported, because somebody's dose is now missing and somebody else could have picked it up.

Then there's storage and labeling, which is where most problems actually start. Medications stay secured, at the right temperature, separated by resident, and never loose in your pocket. They stay in their original labeled container, and the label has to be readable and belong to that resident. Never move medication into a different container. Never give a resident anything from an unlabeled one. And never use one resident's medication for another. Not in an emergency, not when somebody has run out, not for any reason at all.

Documentation goes in on your shift, factually, not from memory at the end of the week. If it wasn't documented, the next person has no way to know whether it happened, and that's how a resident ends up with a doubled dose or a missed one.

And report the small stuff, because you're not expected to make a clinical judgment about any of it. An expiration date. A pill that looks different than usual. A supply about to run out. A label that doesn't match what's inside. You notice, you say something, and somebody qualified decides what it means.
