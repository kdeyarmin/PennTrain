Let's walk through the ones that actually happen, because most emergencies in a residential building aren't dramatic. They're utility failures that become dangerous because of who lives there.

Power loss is the most common. Your building probably has a generator, and you need to know what it carries and what it doesn't. Which outlets are on emergency power and which are not, because that decides whether an oxygen concentrator or a CPAP or a feeding pump keeps running. Know which residents depend on those devices, what the backup is for each one, and how long that backup lasts. Check on those residents first. And remember a power failure can take the heat, the cooling, the elevators, the electronic locks, the call bell system, and the medication refrigerator with it, so it's never just about the lights. And never, ever use candles or an open flame as emergency lighting.

Heat and cold are genuinely dangerous for older adults, who regulate temperature poorly and often won't tell you they're uncomfortable. In heat, watch for confusion, weakness, dizziness, and hot dry skin, get people out of the sun and the warmest rooms, and push fluids the way each care plan allows. In cold, watch for shivering that stops, sleepiness, and confusion, and add layers. In both cases report the change rather than deciding what it is, because those presentations overlap with infection and medication effects.

Water loss means no handwashing, no flushing, no cooking, no bathing. Know where your bottled water, sanitizer, and hygiene supplies are kept. That's the whole preparation.

Severe weather. Know your shelter areas, usually interior rooms away from windows on the lowest safe floor. And be realistic: moving a building full of people who use walkers and wheelchairs takes far longer than any warning gives you. Start early. If you ever evacuate to another site, the three things that matter most are an accurate resident count, medications, and records leaving with the residents.

And then a missing resident, which the field calls elopement, and which is one of the most dangerous events in a building like yours. Prevention is part of every shift: know who's at risk of leaving, know your alarmed doors and secured exits, and never prop a door or disable an alarm because your hands are full. If you can't locate somebody, notify immediately. Do not spend twenty minutes quietly looking and hoping it resolves before anyone finds out. Minutes matter, because a confused resident outside in traffic, in heat, or in cold is in real danger right now.

Across every one of these, the rule is the same: notify, don't investigate. Getting the right people involved fast is your job. Figuring out what happened is theirs.
