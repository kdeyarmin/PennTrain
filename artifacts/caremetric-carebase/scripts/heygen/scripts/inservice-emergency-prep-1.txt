I'm Kevin. More than twenty years in senior care, five of them running a nursing home and seventeen in hospice. And I've been in the building for more of these than I'd like: power out in February, a water main break, a resident missing for forty minutes, a lockdown that turned out to be nothing and one that didn't.

You have a separate course on fire safety, and fire is the emergency everybody trains for. This one is about all the others. The ones that don't have an alarm attached and don't announce themselves.

Pennsylvania requires annual emergency preparedness training for exactly that reason. Your facility has a written emergency plan. That plan only works if the people on shift at two in the morning know what's in it, and in an actual emergency there is no time to read anything.

So let's start with your part of it, because good plans assign duties by shift and by area. Who sweeps which rooms. Who carries the current resident roster. Who grabs medications or the emergency bag. Who meets responders at the door. Who stays with residents at the assembly point. If you can't tell me what yours is for the shift you usually work, ask this week. Not during the event.

Then there's the resident information that changes everything you do, and nobody has time to look it up later. Which residents can't move independently. Which residents depend on electricity to stay well, meaning an oxygen concentrator, a CPAP, a feeding pump, a powered mattress. Which residents wander. Which ones have diabetes or a seizure disorder or dementia that changes how they'll handle disruption, heat, or cold. Which ones can't hear you or can't see you well enough to follow instructions in a dark hallway.

And know your building. Where the utility shutoffs are. Where the flashlights are, and whether they actually work, which you want to find out now instead of in the dark. What the doors do when the power fails, especially the electronically secured ones. Which areas are safest in severe weather. How you'd move residents between floors if the elevators are out.

Last piece of the framing, and it matters: know your limits inside the plan. You are not the incident commander, the fire department, security, or the clinician. Your job is to protect the residents in front of you, follow your assignment, communicate accurately, and get help fast. Improvising outside your assignment is how staff end up in the wrong hallway while somebody's room goes unchecked.
