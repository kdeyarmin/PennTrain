So something is already starting. Here's what actually works, and none of it is complicated.

Give them room. Step back rather than in. Keep your hands visible and low. Approach from the front, where they can see you coming, and get to eye level if you can. If you're standing over somebody in a chair, you are already escalating, before you've said a word.

Slow everything down. Your voice, your movements, your instructions. Short sentences. One idea at a time. Use their name. And then stop talking and let the silence do some work, because people tend to match the tone in the room, and most of us ruin that by filling the quiet with more explanation. More explanation is more pressure.

Agree with the feeling, even when you can't agree with the facts. Arguing with a resident about whether their coat was stolen, or whether their mother is still alive, has never once helped anybody. You are not there to win the disagreement. You're there to get through the next ten minutes without anyone getting hurt.

Offer real choices, and mean them. Now, or after lunch? A person who is fighting for control will very often accept the same care ten minutes later once it was their idea.

Turn down the volume around the situation. The television, the noise, the audience. And send away the extra people who came to help and are now three more voices. One staff member talking works far better than three.

And when it isn't working, walk away if it's safe to do so. Care that isn't urgent can wait. Come back in fifteen minutes, or ask a coworker to try. A resident who tells me no at nine o'clock might say yes to my coworker at nine fifteen, and there is nothing about that that reflects badly on me. Refusing care is a right, not a behavior problem, and forcing it is how people get hurt on both sides. What isn't optional is reporting the refusal, so the team can respond to it.

Keep your own safety in the picture the whole time. Know where the door is. Don't let yourself get cornered. Don't turn your back on an escalating situation. Stay outside of striking range while you work. None of that is confrontational, and none of it is paranoid. It's the thing that lets you stay in the room long enough for the rest of it to work.

One more piece of practical advice from twenty years of watching this. The staff who are best at this aren't the calmest people by nature. They're the ones who decided in advance what they would do, so that they weren't inventing it at the worst possible moment.
