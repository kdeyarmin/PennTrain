I want to close on two things that don't look like infection control until you've watched them play out: immobility, and your own health.

Pennsylvania groups infection prevention and the risks of immobility into the same annual training requirement, and when I first read that years ago I thought it was regulatory housekeeping. It isn't. A resident who stops moving loses muscle within days. They develop pressure injuries over the bony places. They get constipated. And they're far more likely to develop pneumonia, because lungs don't clear well in someone who lies flat and breathes shallowly for weeks.

So repositioning on the schedule the plan sets, getting people up for meals, encouraging the walk to the dining room instead of defaulting to a wheelchair because it's faster, looking at skin while you're already providing care, all of that is infection prevention. It's just infection prevention further upstream, before there's anything to prevent.

Report early, and report changes rather than conclusions. A new cough. A fever. A new area of redness over a bony spot that doesn't fade. Loose stools. A resident who suddenly isn't eating.

And here's the one that catches experienced staff: in older adults, infection often doesn't look like infection. Instead of a fever you get new confusion, unusual sleepiness, agitation in someone who's normally calm, a fall out of nowhere, or a sudden change in continence. Those are the presentations. If you wait for the textbook version, you'll be late. You see these residents every day, which means you are the person who notices the change first, and the day you notice is worth more than the day it becomes obvious to everybody.

If two or more residents or staff come down with something similar, that goes up immediately. That's how an outbreak gets caught while it's still small.

Then there's your own health, and I'll be blunt because I've been on both sides of this. If you're sick, coming in is not loyalty. It's exposure. I have built those schedules, and I know exactly what a call-off does to a shift. And from that side of the desk I'm telling you that one person working through a stomach bug in a building full of frail people costs far more shifts than the one you were trying to cover, and sometimes it costs a resident.

Follow your facility's illness reporting policy. Report an exposure the same day it happens, including a splash or a needlestick, rather than waiting to see how you feel tomorrow.

Do these things consistently and you will personally prevent illnesses you'll never know about. You don't get thanked for those. That's the job.
