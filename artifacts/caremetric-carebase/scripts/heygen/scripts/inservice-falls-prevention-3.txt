Now the hard one. You walk in and somebody's on the floor.

And every instinct you have is screaming get them up. Right now. Before they're embarrassed, before anybody sees, before it turns into a whole thing.

Don't.

That instinct is exactly how a bad afternoon turns into an injury they don't recover from.

So here's what you do instead. Stay with them. If you can call for help without leaving, call. Keep them still, and actually look at them before you move anything.

Ask what happened. Ask if they hit their head. Ask where it hurts, and pay attention to whether the answer stays the same, because a lot of times it doesn't. Look for blood. Look for swelling. Look at their legs, because a leg that's turned out or looks shorter than the other one is a hip, and moving somebody with a broken hip is how this becomes permanent.

Then apply the rule, and the rule is simple. Any sign of injury, any head strike, any complaint about the neck or the back or the hip, any blacking out, or any version of "I don't know what happened" — they stay right where they are and you get help.

Two of these deserve extra caution.

If nobody saw it, treat it as a head injury until somebody qualified says otherwise. A resident telling you they didn't hit their head is not the same as knowing they didn't. They were confused enough to end up on the floor.

And if they're on a blood thinner, a head strike is serious even when there's nothing to see. Bleeding can build for hours. No bruise right after the fall proves absolutely nothing.

Afterward, write down what you saw. Where they were, what position, what they said, what you found, whether anybody witnessed it, what you did, who you told and when. What you saw. Not your theory about it.

And be honest about the conditions. If the floor was wet, if the brake was broken, if the light was out, if the walker was clear across the room, write it down.

I know there's a pull not to. It can feel like you're pointing at a coworker, or at the building. But nobody is helped by a report that quietly leaves it out, and that one detail is the only thing that gets the hazard fixed before it finds the next person.

One last thing, and it matters more than it sounds like.

None of this is about keeping residents in chairs. A resident who never moves gets weaker, and weaker is what causes the next fall.

The goal was never no movement. It's safe movement. Your job is to make the moving they want to do as safe as you can make it, and then let them go do it.
